Chambers & Networking

The low-lift chamber apparel program (a playbook for chamber directors)

By Kristen Coats · July 30, 2026

If you run a chamber of commerce, you already know the request: a member business asks whether the chamber can help them get polos or hats with their logo on them. Or you’re planning a ribbon cutting, a golf tournament, or an ambassador program and someone says “we should have shirts.” The chamber wants to help. The chamber does not want to buy 200 blank polos, store them somewhere, and figure out shipping.

This is the exact situation local embroidery and DTF shops are built for. I’m writing this from the L.I.T. side — we’re a small custom apparel shop in Onalaska, TX, and we run this program with a couple of the chambers around Lake Livingston — but the playbook is portable to any local chamber and any local apparel partner.

What “low-lift” actually means

The version that works looks nothing like a chamber merch store. There’s no inventory. There’s no chamber staff time on Wednesday afternoons packing boxes. There’s no minimum order the chamber has to hit. Here’s what it looks like from the outside:

  1. A member business emails the chamber and says “we’d love branded polos for our staff.”
  2. The chamber replies with one paragraph and one link: “Here’s our local apparel partner — mention the chamber and they’ll take care of you.”
  3. The member and the apparel shop handle it end to end. Design, proof, order, production, pickup.
  4. The chamber never touches a shirt. But when the ribbon gets cut, everybody’s wearing matching gear that has the member’s logo on it and the chamber gets the credit for making it easy.

Total staff time from the chamber: one email template, one relationship, one link. That’s the whole program.

What the chamber gets out of it

  • A useful “yes” for a common member request that used to be an awkward “we’ll ask around.”
  • An upgrade to the visual quality of chamber events. Ambassadors in matching branded polos, ribbon-cutting photos with the whole team in the same shirt, tournament volunteers who look like volunteers instead of a random crowd. That shows up in every chamber social post from now on.
  • A story to tell members. “We negotiated a chamber-member rate with a local shop” is a real, tangible benefit that beats a lot of the intangibles chambers usually list. Members who use it once tend to reorder, and word-of-mouth to other members takes over from there.
  • Local reciprocity. Your apparel partner is (by design) a chamber member themselves. This is money circulating inside your business community instead of leaking off to an out-of-state print-on-demand site.

What to look for in an apparel partner

Not every custom apparel shop is set up to run this cleanly. If you’re picking a partner for your chamber, these are the things that actually matter:

  • Owner-run and local. You want a real person answering the phone who’s willing to build the relationship over time — not a call center. If a member business has a bad experience, you want it to be fixable with one phone call.
  • Both embroidery and DTF. Embroidery for polos, hats, and higher-end pieces. DTF (direct-to-film) printing for tees, tote bags, and anything with a full-color logo or gradient. A shop with only one method will steer every job toward that method, whether it fits or not.
  • No minimums. Some print shops require you to order 24 or 48 pieces at a time. That’s a dealbreaker for chamber members — the two-person accounting firm that wants four polos and the 40-person contractor both need to be able to use the program. Your partner should be genuinely comfortable with orders of one or two pieces.
  • Real turnaround times, honestly quoted. A shop that says “we can do anything in a week” is either lying or about to disappoint your member. Ask what their standard turnaround is (5 to 10 business days is normal), what “rush” costs, and how far out they book during peak seasons like August and December.
  • Free digital proofs before anyone commits. Members should be able to see their logo mocked up on the actual garment, at the actual size, in the actual thread colors, before they spend a dollar. If the shop wants a deposit before proofing, keep looking.
  • A chamber-member rate or perk. Doesn’t have to be a huge discount. A modest chamber-member rate, or a waived logo digitizing fee (usually a $25–50 one-time setup for embroidery), or free local pickup — anything that gives you something specific to say in the email template.

The email template

Here’s the entire chamber-side infrastructure. One template, saved as a canned response, ready to send.

Hi [Member],

We work with [Apparel Shop Name] here in [City] as our local branded-apparel partner. They handle everything from polos and hats to full team uniforms, do both embroidery and DTF printing, and there’s no minimum order — so it doesn’t matter if you need 4 pieces or 40.

Mention that you’re a [Chamber Name] member and they’ll take care of you. Contact: [phone] / [email] / [website].

Let me know if you have any trouble and I’ll loop in [owner name] directly.

— [Chamber Director]

That’s it. That is the entire program. Send that email 15 times a year and the ambassadors are in matching polos, the ribbon-cutting photos look sharp, and your members are getting a real benefit for their dues.
